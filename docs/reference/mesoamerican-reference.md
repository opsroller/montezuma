# Mesoamerican reference

*Reference. Sourced facts about Mesoamerican temple architecture, stone,
stucco, pigment, water, rainforest and light, in the units an artist
authors in. For why the board looks the way it does today, see
[Art direction](../explanation/art-direction.md); for the look each tier
must reach, [Visual target](visual-target.md). This page does not decide
the art direction — it gives the evidence and the constraints the
technical artist, graphic designer and owner decide against.*

## How to read this page

Every claim carries a confidence grade:

| Grade | Means |
|---|---|
| **attested** | a cited source states it directly |
| **inferred** | it follows from cited evidence by the argument given here |
| **contested** | specialists disagree; both positions are named |
| **unknown** | no evidence found; what would settle it is stated |

Numbers without a grade in the same row are **attested**. Citations are
Chicago notes style, keyed to [Sources](#sources).

Reference informs original work. Nothing here is to be traced, modelled
from a photograph of a real monument, or copied; see
[Rights and law](#rights-and-law), which is a hard constraint on this
project, not advice.

## The decision this page settles: highland or lowland

"An Aztec temple in the jungle" is two different places welded together,
and the weld shows. The Mexica (Aztec) built the Templo Mayor in the
Basin of Mexico, on an island in a shallow saline lake at about
2,240–2,276 m above sea level, where mean annual temperature is 16 °C and
rainfall 700–900 mm a year.[^1] Lake-plain sedges and salt flats
surrounded the city; pine, pine–oak and fir forest began on the slopes
*above* it, from about 2,275 m.[^2] There is no rainforest anywhere in
the Basin of Mexico and never was in the Mexica period.

Lowland rainforest sites are a different world, 2,000 m lower and 9 °C
warmer. Palenque sits at 198 m, with a mean annual temperature of
25.3 °C, 80% relative humidity and 5.89 mm of rain a day (≈2,150 mm a
year); Tikal at 279 m, 24.5 °C, 83% humidity, 4.74 mm a day
(≈1,730 mm).[^3] Those are Maya cities, built of sedimentary limestone
and lime plaster, in closed-canopy forest.

So the artist has two honest options, and one dishonest one.

| | **A. Highland Mexica precinct** | **B. Lowland rainforest site** |
|---|---|---|
| Model site | Templo Mayor, Mexico-Tenochtitlan | Palenque, Tikal, Calakmul, Tulum |
| Setting | island city in a shallow lake, open sky, mountains on the horizon | forest clearing; canopy wall at the plaza edge |
| Elevation | ~2,240 m | 150–390 m |
| Stone | volcanic: basalt, tezontle scoria, andesite | sedimentary limestone, as a veneer over a rubble-and-mortar core |
| Surface | lime plaster and stucco over volcanic masonry | thick multi-coat lime plaster, pounded and burnished |
| Palette on stone | **five colours only**: red, wine-red, ochre, Maya blue, white, black[^4] | wider: twenty distinguishable tones at Bonampak, greens included[^5] |
| Vegetation | sedges, reeds, agave, nopal; pine–oak forest on the slopes | 15–25 m closed canopy, ~2,800 stems/ha |
| Water | shallow alkaline lake, canals, stone-lined drains | cenotes and aguadas; almost no rivers or streams |
| Light | thin high-altitude air, hard shadows, clear dry season | hazier; direct-beam irradiance ~19% lower than the highland[^3] |

**The dishonest option is the pastiche**: Mexica iconography (the Templo
Mayor's twin shrines, the Coyolxauhqui disc, serpent heads, the
step-fret) wrapped in Petén rainforest. It is the version almost every
film, poster and match-3 game uses, and it is why the look reads as
generic rather than placed.

### Which one the game is closest to

**Option B, the lowland rainforest site — but built out of Option A's
iconography.** The board today is "weathered limestone under a warm buff
tint," the plaza floor is irregular limestone flags with moss in the
joints, and the backdrop is jungle foliage at `#24521f`; the material
references name Palenque explicitly for how the stone ages.[^6] That is
a Maya lowland material set. The naming and furniture, though, are
Mexica: the pieces are "Aztec totems" on a stone tablet, the frame
carries a step-fret, and the stone is said to hold red-ochre pigment in
its hollows the way Aztec basalt sculpture does.[^6]

The cheapest way to make the look specific is therefore **to finish the
move the materials have already made**: keep the lowland limestone,
plaster and forest, and let the carved furniture be Maya rather than
Mexica — or, if the Mexica reading matters to the brand, swap the
backdrop and the stone for the highland set below. Either is defensible.
The present mixture is the only option that is not.

A theme is a parameter file here, so both are reachable: one theme in
each register is exactly what the theme system exists for.

## Scope, period and names

| | |
|---|---|
| **Highland** | Mexica (Nahuatl-speaking), Mexico-Tenochtitlan, Late Postclassic. Templo Mayor construction stages I–VII, ca. 1325–1520; the imperial style matured between 1440 and 1502.[^7] |
| **Lowland Maya** | Late Classic, ca. 600–900 (Palenque, Tikal, Calakmul, Bonampak); Late Postclassic for Tulum, whose province of Ecab ran 1400–1550.[^8] |

Names used here, with the common name noted once:

- **Mexica** for the people who built Tenochtitlan. "Aztec" is a modern
  umbrella term for Nahua peoples of the Basin; it is fine in
  player-facing text but it is not what they called themselves.
- **Maya** for the peoples of the lowlands; it is a language family and
  a cultural region, not one polity, so "Maya architecture" always needs
  a site and a period attached before it means anything.
- Nahuatl, Yucatec and Spanish terms are given in the spelling the cited
  source uses.

## Option A — Highland Mexica precinct

### Site and setting

| Fact | Value | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Basin of Mexico mean altitude | 2,276 m a.s.l. | attested[^1] |
| Lake high watermark | 2,250 m a.s.l. | attested[^1] |
| Ground near the Templo Mayor (borehole Pc-143 surface) | 2,236.5 m a.s.l. | attested[^9] |
| Original lake system area | 1,280 km² | attested[^1] |
| Mean annual rainfall | 700–900 mm | attested[^1] |
| Mean annual temperature | 16 °C (range 13–25 °C) | attested[^1] |
| Rainy season | summer; up to 5% of rain falls in winter | attested[^1] |
| Grid-cell climatology, Mexico City | 13.8 °C mean; 2.17 mm/day; 51.6% cloud; RH 66% | attested[^3] |
| Night cold | grid-cell minimum −3.4 °C in January | attested[^3] |

The lake was not one body of water. Humboldt's 1804 tests, re-read in
2023, make Texcoco and Chalco denser and more saline than the rest, and
report Chalco's water as clear and colourless, Xochimilco's as
transparent and colourless, and Zumpango's and San Cristóbal's as **grey
and more turbid**.[^1] So the water around the precinct is a pale,
slightly milky grey-green alkaline shallow — not a tropical blue and not
a swamp green.

The island was made ground. To stand about 5 m above the lake floor the
fill mass had to be built roughly 11.6 m thick, because it sank as it
was built; the main fill was earth at about 1.6 t/m³, and the pyramid's
total settlement is of the order of 5.6 m.[^9] One consequence an artist
can use directly: **the plazas and courts of the precinct slope outwards
and downwards**, because compressibility is greater at the perimeter than
under the pyramid.[^9] Floors in the highland precinct are not flat.

### Proportions and sizes

| Element | Value | Grade |
|---|---|---|

| Height, final stage | about 45 m | contested — see below[^10] |
| Height, final stage, structural reconstruction | 36 m | contested[^9] |
| Bodies (stepped tiers) | 4 superimposed, on a quadrangular platform | attested[^10] |
| Shrines on top | 2 — Huitzilopochtli (south), Tlaloc (north) | attested[^10] |
| Stairways | a double stairway, on the west façade only | attested[^10] |

| Implied riser | 0.26–0.33 m | inferred — see below |
| Sacred precinct area | 12.24 ha | attested[^11] |
| Island capital area, early 16th century | 13.5 km² | attested[^11] |

**The height is contested.** López Luján gives "around 45 metres,"
comparing it to the Pyramid of the Moon at Teotihuacan.[^10] Mazari,
Marsal and Alberro, reconstructing the six main stages in order to
compute settlement, assume 36 m for stage VI and argue that the
asymmetric load of the western stairways was part of why the Mexica did
not push the height further.[^9] Both are reconstructions from the
stumps the Spaniards left at floor level; neither is a measurement of a
standing building. Treat 36–45 m as the range.

**Riser, inferred.** 120 steps is attested from the historical
sources.[^9] The stair does not climb to the 36–45 m apex — that figure
includes the shrines and their roofs — but to the terrace the shrines
stand on. With the stair top between about 31 m and 40 m, 120 steps give
a riser of 0.26–0.33 m. A riser near 0.30 m with a tread near 0.30 m
gives a pitch close to 45°, which is the character the sources describe
and a sound proportion to author at. **The tread itself is unknown**: I
found no excavation figure for the run of a Templo Mayor step. A measured
section of the surviving Stage II stair, the best-preserved part of the
building, would settle it.

Mexica bodies carry a steep battered face (*talud*), and the stairways
are flanked by heavy sloped balustrades (*alfardas*). Serpent heads
carved in basalt sit at the foot of the balustrades, and the pyramid's
bodies carry deliberately coarse protruding stones, read as the "wild"
character of *tepetl*, hill.[^12] That last detail is a gift to a
procedural stone generator: **the rough stones are intentional, not
decay.**

### Stone, and how each weathers

Five rock types are recorded in excavation, with their Nahuatl names and
their colours:[^10]

| Stone | Nahuatl | Colour as recorded | Used for | Authoring value (inferred) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dense basalt | *metlatetl* | grey and black | foundations, load-bearing walls, stairways, exterior floors | `#3a3b38` |
| Volcanic scoria | *tezontli* (tezontle) | reddish, violet or blackish | fill, façades, stairways, stucco floors, paving, walls | `#7a4038` / `#4a3a3e` / `#332f2e` |
| Lamprobolite andesite | *tenayocatetl* | pale pinkish | floor slabs, corner stones, facing ashlars, drains | `#b99a90` |
| Pyroxene andesite | *iztapaltetl* | grey or off-white | floors, foundation templates, façade ties, drains | `#9e9d96` |
| Limestone | *tetizatl* | whitish, grey, rose and ochre | luxury tile floors in two small rooms flanking the upper steps, stage IVa only; imported from what is now Puebla | `#c9bfae` |

The colour *words* are attested; the hex values are **inferred** — my
rendering of those words for authoring, not a measurement. The
lamprobolite andesite's "pseudostratification makes it easy to obtain
flat faces," which is why it becomes slabs and ashlars[^10]: a generator
should give it flat, parallel-bedded faces and give the basalt blockier,
tighter ones.

Why volcanic and not sedimentary: the Basin had no outcrops of
sedimentary or metamorphic rock, and without draught animals or wheeled
transport the Mexica did not move large blocks across the surrounding
mountains.[^10] Canoe transport was 40–50 times more efficient than
human carriers, and material moved at 2.6–3.5 km/h whether carried or
paddled.[^10]

Weathering to author, by material:

- **Tezontle** is highly vesicular. It takes dirt and biofilm into its
  pores and goes darker and flatter with age rather than developing a
  polished surface. *(inferred from its description as "light but highly
  resistant volcanic slag"[^10]; no weathering study of tezontle was in
  my sources.)*
- **Basalt** holds a carved edge and keeps a dense surface. In museum
  condition it reads grey, pink or violet, and that bare state is exactly
  what makes modern viewers imagine Mexica sculpture as unpainted.[^4]
- **Lime plaster and stucco** covered floors, walls and stairways at
  every stage of the building,[^10] so bare stone showing is the
  *ruined* state, not the built one.

### Stucco and pigment: the five-colour rule

This is the single most useful finding on this page for the look of the
board.

The palette on Mexica monumental sculpture and mural painting is
**limited to red, wine-red, ochre, blue, white and black**. Every colour
but the black was applied **directly onto the roughness of the rock, with
no ground or preparation layer**, forming "monochromatic, well-delimited,
saturated, opaque, uniform surfaces with no change of tone and no
shadows."[^4]

| Colour | Material, by X-ray diffraction | Where used on the Tlaltecuhtli monolith | Authoring value (inferred) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red | hematite, well crystallised and finely ground | blood, gums, ears, leather, banners, the background | `#9b2a1c` |
| Wine red | hematite plus a low percentage of titanomagnetite, which darkens it | hair | `#5c1c18` |
| Ochre | goethite with poorly crystallised hematite — yellow ochre | the goddess's skin | `#c98a3c` |
| Maya blue | palygorskite clay (*sacalum*) plus indigo from *Indigofera suffruticosa* leaves | ear and facial ornaments, Venus glyphs, eyebrows of skulls | `#2f6f8f` |
| White | calcite | cotton, paper, bone, teeth, claws, stars, shells | `#ede8dc` |
| Black | non-crystalline; pine-soot ink, *tlilli ocotl* | outlines of cotton textiles, bones, stars | `#23201c` |

The minerals are **attested** by diffractometry.[^4] The hex values are
**inferred** authoring starting points from those mineral
identifications; I found no published colorimetric measurement of these
surfaces, and anyone calling these values accurate should get one first.

The binder is not a gum, protein, oil, wax or resin: gas chromatography
–mass spectrometry found sugars, specifically glucose and mannose, i.e.
**orchid mucilage**, pressed from the pseudobulbs of orchids endemic to
the Basin and Morelos.[^4] A matte, slightly chalky, non-glossy paint
film follows.

What the Mexica did **not** paint on stone, lime or earth: green,
orange, brown or grey. Those colours exist in the codices — a visual
examination of the Codex Borbonicus counted seventeen (bright red,
bright pink, pale pink, orange, yellow, mauve, dark violet-blue, pale
grey-blue, turquoise blue, dark green, pale green, dark grey-brown,
chocolate brown, black, dark brown-grey, pale grey and white) — but not
on the architecture. Whether that is because such paints did not survive
weather, were too costly for large surfaces, or carried symbolic meaning
is openly unresolved.[^13]

**The precinct's colour is organised, not decorative.** On stage IVb the
two serpents bounding the platform on the west are one blue (north,
cold, rain, wet season) and one ochre (south, warm, sun, fire, dry
season), and the serpent on the central axis is **half blue and half
ochre**. The four serpent heads at the stair feet, the heads on the side
and rear façades, and the masonry braziers beside them repeat the split:
Maya blue on the Tlaloc half, goethite ochre on the Huitzilopochtli
half.[^14] The serpent bodies are masonry covered in stucco, then
painted.[^14]

If the board wants to say "Mexica" in one gesture, this is it: **a
north–south blue/ochre split across one symmetrical building**, not a
scatter of colours.

### Water

- The lake: shallow, alkaline, pale grey-green to colourless, turbid in
  the northern lakes.[^1]
- Pyroxene and lamprobolite andesite were both used for **drains**,[^10]
  and a water collector is part of the excavated precinct.
- Fill soil came from shallow lake beds and marshy shores: clay-textured,
  **dark brown** from its organic content, carrying lake and marsh seeds,
  fish scales and freshwater snails.[^10] A cut or eroded edge in the
  highland precinct exposes dark brown earth with shell in it, not pale
  rubble.

data; a Basin-specific stand survey would be better and I did not find
one.

For the board's backdrop the practical point is the **silhouette**:
conifer spires and hard-leaved oaks on blue-grey distant slopes, agave
rosettes and nopal on the dry piedmont, reed beds at the water. Nothing
broad-leaved, nothing with a buttress root, nothing with a liana.

## Option B — Lowland rainforest site

### Site and setting

| Site | Elevation | Mean temp | RH | Rain | Cloud | Direct-beam irradiance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Palenque (17.48 °N, 92.05 °W) | 198 m | 25.3 °C | 80% | 5.89 mm/day (≈2,150 mm/yr) | 58.6% | 4.16 kWh/m²/day |
| Tikal (17.22 °N, 89.62 °W) | 279 m | 24.5 °C | 83% | 4.74 mm/day (≈1,730 mm/yr) | 61.9% | 3.65 kWh/m²/day |
| Los Tuxtlas (18.58 °N, 95.07 °W) | 97 m | 25.3 °C | 82% | 5.86 mm/day | 59.5% | 3.36 kWh/m²/day |
| *Mexico City, for contrast* | *2,627 m (grid)* | *13.8 °C* | *66%* | *2.17 mm/day* | *51.6%* | *4.53 kWh/m²/day* |

All attested, 25-year climatology, January 2001 – December 2025.[^3]
Calakmul, the measured forest in the vegetation section below, sits at
260–385 m with 24.6 °C and 1,076 mm a year.[^120]

Two things follow for the look, and both are directly authorable:

1. **The dry season is the crisp season.** At Palenque, March is the
   least cloudy month (41.4%) with the year's highest direct-beam
   irradiance (4.99 kWh/m²/day) and the least rain (1.78 mm/day); April
   is the hottest (28.8 °C mean) and humidity bottoms out at 59%. By
   September cloud is 74.1% and rain 11.5 mm/day.[^3] A sunlit temple
   with hard shadows is a **late-dry-season (March–April)** scene; a
   wet-season scene is flat, grey and streaming.
2. **Lowland light is hazier than highland light.** Tikal receives about
   19% less *direct-beam* irradiance than Mexico City while receiving
   only 10% less total light (**inferred** from the attested figures in
   [Haze and aerial perspective](#haze-and-aerial-perspective)). The
   difference is scattered light. The highland, not the jungle, is the
   place for a hard, thin, high-contrast key.

### The surface is the asset, not the stonework

This is the finding that should change the board most.

Loten, writing the Tikal North Acropolis report, states plainly that "no
facing masonry of finish surfaces was ever left exposed… all were
covered with plaster," and that every structure in the report
"require[s] relatively thick plaster for finishing."[^15] Plaster was
made by grinding or dissolving limestone and letting it set again; cuts
through it show curving pressure lines, which he reads as the plaster
having been pounded to densify it, after which "exterior plaster
surfaces were pounded, rubbed and burnished… This would have rendered
freshly completed structures somewhat reflective and glossy."[^16]

And the plaza:

> "the Great Plaza surface, now grass, was then polished white plaster.
> The structures around the plaza glistened with a plaster coat that had
> been either painted or burnished. Restored masonry surfaces, now
> mottled black, give an entirely false impression of the original
> character. Even on an overcast [day] such as that of Plate 3 the scene
> would have been quite bright, and in full sunlight, blinding."[^17]

He adds that the glare would have made the plaza "extremely
uncomfortable" when new, diminishing as it weathered, and that whether
the glare was intended is moot.[^18] Grade: the burnishing and the
pressure lines are **attested** observation; "pounded to harden it" and
the glare's intent are Loten's own **inferred** readings, and he marks
them as such ("My impression", "I think", "moot").

About half the North Acropolis structures carry **red paint, the pigment
most commonly specular hematite**; other colours, on sculptural features
only, are cream, blue, black and green.[^19] At Group SE-11 the exterior
walls retain red-paint traces that probably covered the whole outer
wall.[^20] Absence is recorded too: several structures show no trace of
paint at all.[^21]

**The implication for the board.** Modelling crisp stone blocks and
leaving them grey inverts the evidence twice over: the blocks were not
meant to be seen, and the surface that was meant to be seen was a glossy,
near-white or red lime skin. Given this project's preference for stock
Godot materials over custom shaders, that reads as a **low-roughness
white-to-cream plaster material with a red variant**, not as a tiled
stone texture. The grey, block-faced ruin is the *modern* condition, and
a restored-masonry grey is, in Loten's words, "an entirely false
impression."

### Proportions, in the units a generator takes

**Terrace pitch is a free parameter, by design.** Loten: by dividing a
platform into terraces "it was possible to adjust the angle of each to
ensure that the top surface area would not be smaller than desired,"
which also kept apron mouldings to a manageable height.[^22] There is no
canonical Maya terrace angle to hit; the builders drove pitch from a
target summit footprint. A procedural generator can legitimately do the
same.

The only measured batter in the corpus is **0.06 m in 1.00 m of height**
(≈3.4° off vertical) — and Loten suspects that one is outward
displacement by tree roots, not design.[^23] Do not ship it as a design
batter. Qualitatively, batter at Tikal ranges from "pronounced" through
"moderate" and "slight" to "nearly vertical" and "no batter," varying
between structures and between terraces of one structure.[^24]

| Element | Figure | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Height : width of one temple-type structure (Str. 6B-30), without roof comb | 1 : 1.43 (height = 70% of width) | attested[^25] |
| Substructure share of that total height | 62% | attested[^25] |
| Building width : depth, same structure | 2.4 : 1 | attested[^25] |
| Range-type structure (Str. 5D-105): height as share of basal length | 39% — "roughly a little more than three times as long as it is high" | attested[^26] |
| Room height | about 5 m | attested[^27] |
| Room width : height | 1 : 2.86 — "heights are nearly three times the width" | attested[^27] |
| Implied room width at 5 m high | about 1.75 m | inferred from[^27] |
| Vault height : wall height | 1 : 1.57 | attested[^27] |
| Vault spring | outset 0.06–0.10 m, at wall-top level | attested[^28] |
| Vault courses | seven, plus a small levelling course under the capstones | attested[^29] |
| Capstone length / width | ca. 1 m long; 0.14–0.30 m wide; mostly pre-plastered | attested[^30] |
| Wall thickness | 1.65 m at the front to more than 5 m at the rear | attested[^31] |
| Lintel bed below wall top | 0.40–0.50 m | attested[^32] |
| Stair bulge width (one case) | approximately 22 m | attested[^33] |

A room 5 m high and 1.75 m wide is a **slot**. Maya interiors are tall,
narrow and dark; the mass is in the walls, not the void. If the game ever
shows an interior, that proportion is the single most characteristic
thing about it.

**Corbel or not, by period:** North Acropolis (Early Classic) vaults use
"relatively unspecialized masonry units" and "corbelled vaulting" is the
right term for them; **Late Classic vaulting uses specialized masonry
units that are not corbelled**.[^34] A remake set in the Late Classic
should cut vault stones to shape, not step plain blocks.

**Front and back are not the same.** Vaulted North Acropolis structures
always carry a band of masonry above wall level that projects beyond the
wall face, and "frontal upper zones were carved while rearward ones were
left plain."[^35] Carve the façade; leave the back flat. Stairs changed
too: an early axial stair is "fully inset," which "contrasts with outset
stairs on later works."[^36] Corners are sometimes rounded, sometimes
sharp, and both occur at Tikal.[^37]

**Apron mouldings are plaster, not cut stone.** The Third Supplementary
Platform's apron mouldings are "formed largely of stucco… with only
slight working of the facing stones"; the aprons on Platform 5D-4-9th-B
are "modeled in stucco."[^38] An apron moulding is "a terrace profile
formed by projecting an upper portion of the terrace face out beyond the
lower," sometimes with the sub-apron reading as a horizontal slot and
the apron profile slightly convex.[^39] For a generator: **model the
moulding in the plaster layer, over roughly-shaped stone.** Tikal does
not use Teotihuacan's *talud-tablero*; the apron moulding is its
equivalent. (Where *talud-tablero* does occur, the Teotihuacan tablero is
larger than the talud at about 3:1, while at Kaminaljuyu the two are
equal at 1:1 — do not transplant either onto a Petén temple.)[^40]

*(The printed table's standard-deviation column is internally

means are 0.17 m; they read as centimetres. Reported as printed, not
silently rescaled.)*

**The scale rule that matters:** "Wall facings are made of relatively
small stones. Terrace facing stones are much larger and more regular in
shape."[^15] A pyramid's terraces take big regular blocks and the
building on top takes small ones. A single uniform block size across a
whole structure is wrong.

Cores and fill, all attested:

- "Wall cores consist of soft white limestone scraps, not bedded, in
  dark gray mortar."[^47]
- "Wall core includes hard flint nodules and broken flint rubble in a
  dark matrix of muddy mortar."[^48]
- Core masonry elsewhere includes large dressed stones about
  0.50 × 0.30 × 0.10 m bedded in dark muddy mortar with aggregate up to
  0.10–0.20 m.[^49]
- Two extremes of core, loose and bedded; high structures use coursed
  core bedded in mortar, which is "so inherently stable that no retaining
  walls are needed at the outer faces."[^50]
- Mortars range from "mere earth, mud or clay" to "almost pure lime
  similar in composition to the best plasters"; the better mortars go in
  the facings, and "inner core material generally displays weaker
  mortar."[^51]

Dressing order varies and is visible: some blocks were "dressed after
installation to produce a regular wall face," others "dressed to a smooth
finish before the stones were set in the wall and not afterward."[^52]
"Tool marks from hard stone mauls are visible in many stone faces,"
which Loten reads as two specialised crews, cutters and setters.[^53]

### Lintels

Ten to fifteen **unworked round beams** per lintel, 0.08–0.18 m in
diameter, butts extending 0.85–0.90 m beyond the door jambs and concealed
behind masonry housings plastered flush with the wall; one surviving beam
still retained its bark.[^62] There are **no stone lintels at
Tikal**.[^63]

**The species is not securely identified.** The reports call the beams
"logwood" throughout, with a single parenthesised "(zapote?)" — the
report's own question mark.[^64] Elsewhere the same corpus uses
"logwood" as a plant name, for the *Haematoxylum campechianum* that
dominates bajos.[^65] Whether Loten means that species or simply "log
wood" is not resolvable from these reports. Grade: **unknown**. Authoring
line: *unworked round beams, 0.08–0.18 m diameter, species not securely
identified.* Sapodilla (*Manilkara zapota*) is the species usually named
in general accounts of Maya lintels, but it is not what these reports
say, and this page will not put it in their mouth.

### Lime plaster, from the stone up

Lothrop's summary of the whole material economy: Yucatecan buildings are
made of the peninsula's limestone, and "this stone was burned to obtain
lime, crushed to form rubble, and cut to make building-blocks; of it,
the walls, floors, vaults, and all other parts of a house could be
made."[^74] One rock, three products.

**Aggregate.** Petrography of Lamanai plasters identifies the
sub-rounded aggregate as **sascab**, "a calcareous sediment that
comprises a range of particle sizes, from clays to boulders, which is
formed by in situ weathering of limestones and which is abundant in the
karstic terrain of the Maya lowlands."[^75] The angular aggregate is
crushed limestone, added "in order to obtain an interlocking effect,
providing the plasters with better mechanical properties."[^76] Sascab
remained the main aggregate through the Early and Late Classic,[^77] and
its use is corroborated independently by Landa's sixteenth-century
chronicle.[^78] Bulk composition is over 92% CaCO₃.[^79]

Sascab also went in **unburnt**, as tamped floors: the silt and clay
fractions were used as tamped powder for laying floors in the
Preclassic, Early and Late Classic, where "hardness was probably obtained
solely by tamping the fine calcareous sediments when wet, as is still
practiced today in the region for the construction of sascab roads."
Tamped sascab was used in floors only, because it performs adequately
for floors but not for wall renders or sculpture.[^80]

Later periods change: from the Terminal Classic, larger crystalline
calcite aggregate prevails over micritic calcite (read as quarrying
waste), with considerable quartz; from the Late Postclassic, silicon and
aluminium rise (up to 12% SiO₂, 6% Al₂O₃), and devitrified glass
fragments and higher hardness suggest deliberately hydraulic
plasters.[^81]

**Coats.** The rule is attested and is a direct instruction to a material
author: *the rougher the stonework, the thicker the levelling coat.*
Rough vault stonework produced "una superficie del intradós muy
irregular" covered with "una gruesa capa de mortero de regularización de
varios centímetros de espesor," over which the final stucco render went;
Petén vault stones needed only one render coat; finely cut Uxmal
boot-shaped stones left a surface "totalmente lisa," needing only "una
fina capa de estuco de revestimiento."[^82] The photogrammetric study
agrees: Uxmal's stones are "so carefully dressed that only a thin layer
of plaster would have been required to achieve an extremely smooth
finished surface."[^83]

**Repeated repainting.** In sheltered places "as many as six successive
layers of plaster are seen, each application in turn having received a
coat of red, blue or green paint."[^84] *(Attested, but note: this is
Lothrop quoting Holmes (1895) on the sculptured column at San Miguel,
Cozumel — not Tulum, and not Lothrop's own observation.)* The useful
point stands: a surface is a stack of replastering and repainting
events, not one coat.

**What is not sourced.** Despite looking for them, I found **no** burning
temperature for limestone to quicklime, **no** aggregate-to-lime
proportion, **no** numeric aggregate grain size, **no** millimetre
thickness for any coat beyond "varios centímetros," **no** colour for
sascab, and **no** evidence at all for tree bark (*chukum*, *holol*) as a
plaster additive — every apparent "bark" hit in these sources is
"embarked." Bark additives are widely repeated in popular accounts; they
are not in anything I read. Treat them as **unknown** and, if the project
wants the effect, record it as a deliberate departure.

### Vault geometry

Every precise figure below comes from **one photogrammetrically surveyed
room**: room 1 of the House of the Turtles, Uxmal, Classic Puuc. It is
one well-measured case, not a regional average.[^85]

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Room | 9.03 m long × 2.68 m wide, 5.00 m interior height to the underside of the capstones |
| Doorways | 1.50 m wide, opposite each other at the middle of the long sides |
| Step into the rear room | 20 cm high |
| Vault | straight-sided, slope **64° above the horizontal** |
| Vault spring | 2.75 m above the floor, with a 6–7 cm overhang |
| Corbel course below the capstones | 15 cm high, very carefully cut rectangular corbels |
| Capstone span closed | 46 cm |
| Walls | 70 cm thick, seven even courses of well-cut squared stones with rounded backs |
| End-vaults | leaned 2° inward from the vertical |
| Wall : vault share of interior height | 2.75 m wall to 2.25 m vault ≈ 1.22 : 1 (**inferred** arithmetic from the two figures above) |

**Do not transplant these onto Tulum.** Tulum is Late Postclassic
east-coast work that Lothrop calls, bluntly, "very bad masonry."[^86]
Its vaults differ in kind: "The usual shape shows slightly arched sides.
A form peculiar to the east coast has a double curve, which, in
cross-section, suggests the outline of a bottle,"[^87] and "The small
offset at the spring of the vault which is customarily found in Maya
buildings is rarely seen on the east coast."[^88] One Tulum vault springs
"almost from the floor" in a room formerly 9 ft high and 9 ft wide.[^89]
East-coast capstones "are not so nicely fitted as elsewhere, nor are they
painted, as was sometimes done in central Yucatan."[^90] **Wooden beams
at half the height of the vault are common** throughout the Maya
area.[^91]

The vault stones (*dovelas*) run through seven named types from least to
most worked; only the Petén type is given numerically: **25–35 cm base,
30–40 cm high on the exposed face, 30–60 cm deep**, with a rounded and
sometimes roughed-out back to key the outer leaf into the rubble-and-
mortar fill.[^92] Puuc boot-shaped (*bota*) stones are an "L" in section
with a tenon on the back, exposed faces "very smoothly dressed with sharp
borders and perfectly squared corners, so that the joints between them
result in very straight and thin lines."[^85]

**Corbel to cast.** The development runs from corbelled vaults built of
unspecialised units to "monolithic or cast vaults… with highly
specialized vault stones forming the facing of a supporting core of lime
concrete," the facing ending up as "lo que podría considerarse un
encofrado permanente del relleno" — effectively permanent formwork for
the fill.[^93] A Late Classic or Puuc temple is a **concrete shell with a
cut-stone skin**, not a stack of corbels.

### Masonry: a skin on a core

> "It must be remembered that true masonry scarcely exists in Yucatan.
> The usual construction is monolithic in character: a rubble core
> consisting of irregular stones grouted with mortar, on the exterior of
> which is a veneer of cut stone. The stone blocks are cut rectangular
> (in the better class of construction) on the outer face and are set in
> the rubble by a projecting tenon. If the stone is regarded only as a
> veneer and is without structural value, it follows that there is no
> need for breaking joints, indeed no need for courses or any of the
> devices of masonry."[^94]

That last sentence is the most actionable masonry fact on this page.
**East-coast facing does not break joints and does not keep courses.** A
generator that lays a neat running bond is modelling Puuc or Petén work,
not Tulum work. Puuc work, by contrast, *is* laid "in very even
courses."[^85] Two different procedural rules, by region and period.

Wall thickness: east coast "from 1 to 4 feet thick, 2 feet being perhaps
an average figure," which Lothrop notes is thin for Maya walls — thin
enough that the stonework has to carry load and so "assumes the character
of true masonry in many instances."[^86] Uxmal: 70 cm.[^85] The two
happen to agree closely (2 ft ≈ 0.61 m) despite different regions and
build quality.

Floors "were made of polished cement."[^95] Roofs "appear flat and are
coated with a smooth layer of cement."[^96] East-coast roofs and ceilings
"were often formed of wood covered with rubble."[^97]

**Ordinary facing-block dimensions are unknown** in these sources. The
Petén vault-stone range above bounds the size of worked stone in that
region and is the honest substitute.

### Tulum: the outward-leaning wall

Tulum is the lowland site whose silhouette a game most often borrows, so
its peculiarities are worth separating out.

> "A feature particularly developed at Tulum is the outward slope of the
> exterior walls from bottom to top. Its occurrence is too frequent and
> too pronounced to be merely accidental. As a matter of fact, this
> batter is an element of strength, for the outward slope of the exterior

And the detail that is easy to get backwards: "when the walls have an
outward batter the door may slope inward towards the top at a

### Painted stucco: flat fields, black lines

The most directly usable material specification on this page.

> "Large, flat color areas of blue, red, and ochre are arranged on a
> plain stucco background and are outlined with heavy black lines
> measuring from 3-6 millimeters in thickness."[^106]

Plate captions give further outline widths: 4 mm around one Tulum figure
(78 × 54 cm), averaging 8 mm around feather ornaments in another
room.[^107] So: **black outlines 3–8 mm wide, around flat fields of red,
ochre and blue, on a plain stucco ground.**

The palette is regionally graded, and stated explicitly: Xelha gets "a
richer palette… cerulean blue, ochre, two reds, white, and purple, as
opposed to simpler red, ochre, blue, and black at Tancah and
Tulum."[^108] Tulum Structure 16, the Temple of the Frescoes: the outer
shell and superstructure "were originally painted in bright pigments of
two reds, yellow-ochre, blue, and black," while the inner building was
"painted in monochromes of blue on a black ground."[^109]

Lothrop's own colour observations at Tulum: a sunken doorway panel
"painted a turquoise blue outlined in black, with a simple geometrical
pattern in white in the center," and — a gift for a themed board — "The
north half of the room to the height of the spring of the vault is
painted red and the south half is blue."[^110] Lothrop raises the
directional reading and then undercuts it himself: "Red is usually
assigned to the north and black to the south, although the evidence is
conflicting and in certain cases these colors are reversed."[^110]
Grade: **contested** — a north/south colour split is attested as
observed; its directional meaning is not settled.

Stucco relief at Tulum carried "traces of orange, red, and black paint";
one giant corner mask "was once painted orange, red, and black"; one
figure is outlined by "a band of blue paint 3 inches wide."[^111] The
exterior of the Temple of the Frescoes is "as richly decorated in stucco
as is the interior in painting," its west panel carrying "many imprints
of the Red Hand, spaced about a foot apart" — corroborated independently
by INAH, which records human handprints in red on the exterior
façade.[^112] Mouldings carry "numerous rosettes in stucco."[^113] One
floor at Tulum (structure 16) was painted red; Lothrop guesses this "may
have been a universal custom, but to-day the floors are usually too
weathered to tell" — the one floor is **attested**, the generalisation is
his **inferred** guess.[^95]

**"Maya blue" is not identified at Tulum.** The term appears once in all
the lowland sources I read, hedged by its own authors as "probably
composed of Maya blue," and for **Lamanai, Belize**, not Tulum.[^114]
Lothrop's word for the blue he saw is "turquoise blue." Do not label a
Tulum blue "Maya blue" on this evidence.

For the Maya painters' full range, Bonampak is the measured case: twenty
distinguishable tones, with mineral identifications — calcite white,
hydrated iron oxide yellow, hematite-plus-calcite pink, maghemite
red-orange, goethite-and-hematite red-brown skin, carbon-black outlines,
*chapopote* (bitumen) black, Maya blue (indigo + attapulgite) alone and
mixed with azurite, malachite for the blue-greys, and an organic green
that is 100% organic with no mineral.[^5] Paint films are thin: a
yellow preparation film of about 15 microns under a 25-micron dark blue;
a sky-blue layer of about 65 microns; a 20-micron pale blue under a
30-micron darker blue as two cleanly separated strata.[^5] Tones were
built both by glazing one colour over another and by mixing pigments,
which the authors read as deliberate pictorial searching rather than
accident.[^5]

### How plaster and stone weather, in the lowlands

The single best line for the stripped look:

> "In the days when these cities flourished, this was not apparent to the
> eye, for all walls were covered with a smooth coat of stucco; but
> to-day it is sadly obvious, for the stucco has peeled off for the most
> part, revealing the crudeness of the stonework, and the walls
> themselves are often badly cracked."[^86]

And the best line for what is left on a sheltered painted surface:

> "They are covered with mold and a limestone deposit caused by sweating
> of the plaster, and they can not be clearly seen in the obscure light.
> The plaster on which they are painted is now dry and crumbles rather
> readily."[^115]

Three layers to author on an old lowland plaster surface, all attested:
**mould growth**, a **recrystallised carbonate crust** ("limestone
deposit caused by sweating"), and a **dry, friable plaster body** that
crumbles. Where plaster is gone, the crude rubble-and-veneer stonework
behind it is exposed and cracked.

Shelter decides survival. The Temple of the Frescoes murals survived
"because they are protected from the weather by the surrounding
portico"; an inner wall's frescoes are "more faded"; exposed figures are
"almost obliterated"; stucco faces, once "not an uncommon decoration,"
have "completely crumbled away almost everywhere."[^116] **Paint survives
in the lee, under overhangs and in hollows, and nowhere else** — which is
exactly the rule the board's stone already applies to its red-ochre
traces, and the right one.

**Dissolution and recrystallisation are not described as processes** in
anything I read, and **no source states that a specific pigment faded to
a specific other colour**. If the project wants a pigment-shift effect,
it is an invention, not a documented one.

### How the ruin actually looks

The one-sentence summary, from the Tikal report's preface:

> "They were fabricated almost entirely in masonry and many were adorned
> with elaborately sculptured facades. Tree roots have destroyed most of
> the surface detail, wooden lintels have rotted, large sections of
> vaulting have fallen, and bat dung covers many floors."[^66]

Details that make a ruin read as a real one rather than a set:

- **The facing goes; the core stays.** Outer facing had largely fallen
  long before excavation began, "leaving core masonry exposed and still
  firmly in place after centuries of exposure."[^50] The signature
  silhouette is a **stripped stepped core with no skin**, not a neat
  stepped pyramid missing a few blocks.
- **Debris slopes have direction.** A steep debris slope running rear to
  front was itself the evidence that the front wall had collapsed
  completely while the rear wall stood at near full height.[^67]
- **Two collapse episodes are readable** in section: "final collapse
  debris" over "initial collapse debris."[^68]
- **The bottom survives best**, protected by its own talus.[^69]
- **Roots displace, they do not just crack.** A terrace face's 0.06 m in
  1.00 m lean is read as root-driven outward displacement;[^23] wall faces
  are "vertical where not distorted by structural failure or root
  invasion."[^70]
- **Erosion removes plaster first.** "Erosion has removed all traces of
  plaster from the vault"; at the rear of one building the facing eroded
  away entirely, "exposing core masonry of horizontally bedded
  cushion-shaped blocks set in good quality mortar."[^71]
- **Bat guano is corrosive** — one surface is "probably eroded by the
  acid in bat dung," and one structure is nicknamed the "Bat Palace" for
  the layer of it.[^72]
- **Flat humus on top.** A building's lost roof line was reconstructable
  from "the flat humus layer that extends across the building top."[^73]
- **Fewer trees than you think, where it was in use.** Loten: "It is
  unlikely that there would have been so many trees so close to the
  plaza."[^17]

So the game has to choose a moment, and the two moments look opposite:
**in use** — blinding burnished white and red plaster, swept plaza, few
trees; **abandoned** — stripped grey-brown core, debris slopes, root
damage, canopy closed over the top. The board today is somewhere between
the two with neither one's logic.

### Biofilm: what actually colours old limestone

Measured at Chichén Itzá.[^122] This is the most directly usable
weathering data on the page, because it says *which colours go where*.

> "Two major types of biofilm colonize buildings at Chichen Itza, namely
> black biofilms (more abundant) and pink biofilms. These have been shown
> to be dominated by cyanobacteria… and the chlorophyte
> *Trentepohlia*."[^122]

So: **black** (dominant, cyanobacteria) and **pink** (the green alga
*Trentepohlia*, whose carotenoids read orange-pink). Not green. The
project's current plaza moss is a different organism in a different
place — moss in damp joints is fine, but the *faces* of lowland limestone
go black and pink, not green.

And the mask that drives it:

> "This type of microbial biofilm dominates the soiling coverage of
> external limestone surfaces in the Mayan region… and is particularly
> conspicuous in North-oriented walls, sloped surfaces, or in vertical
> areas where water is available most of the time through
> run-off."[^122]

The figure caption is even blunter: "The microbial growth is restricted
to surfaces where run-off water is available. Differential colonization
here evidenced by the different degrees of blackening."[^122]

**Procedural rule, straight out of that:** drive the biofilm mask from
(a) north-facing orientation, (b) surface slope, and (c) simulated
run-off paths — not from cavity occlusion or uniform noise. Blackening is
*differential*, with clean stone between the stained runs.

Underneath the biofilm there is a mineral front. XRD of the
stone–biofilm interface identifies **calcite as the main phase, along
with whewellite and weddellite**[^122] — the two calcium oxalate
hydrates, i.e. a whitish crust produced by the fungi themselves. Fungi
reach 10⁵ colony-forming units per gram of stone; the cultivable
community is dominated by Hypocreales (48%) and Xylariales (24%), with
ten genera confirmed molecularly, including *Xylaria*, *Rosellinia*,
*Annulohypoxylon*, *Cladosporium*, *Fusarium* and *Penicillium*.[^122]

Three layers, then, on an exposed lowland limestone face: **black
cyanobacterial film on the run-off tracks and north faces; pink
*Trentepohlia* patches; a pale calcite-and-oxalate crust at the
interface** — over the stone's own colour.

*(No named lichen or moss genus appears in that study, and it gives no
percentage cover for any wall; both are **unknown**.)*

## The rainforest, in numbers an environment artist can plant with

semi-evergreen tropical forest (SETF) and five in seasonally flooded

| Maximum height in plot (m) | 18.40 ± 3.46 | 13.46 ± 0.68 |
| Clear bole height (m) | 7.04 ± 0.26 | 4.93 ± 0.53 |
| Stem density (ind./ha, **DBH ≥ 5 cm**) | 2,816 ± 355 | 2,428 ± 225 |
| Quadratic mean DBH (cm) | 12.31 ± 0.37 | 11.25 ± 0.71 |

| Leaf area index (m²/m²) | 3.29 ± 0.28 | 3.03 ± 0.36 |

A second source, for the Calakmul Biosphere Reserve as a whole: "These
are forests with trees reaching 15–25 m in height, 25%–50% of which lose
their leaves during the dry season."[^120]

**What this means on the ground.** 2,816 stems per hectare at DBH ≥ 5 cm
is about **2.8 stems per 10 m²** — roughly 280 trunks in a 100 × 10 m
strip. Clear bole height around 7 m means **the first 7 m of a trunk is
bare**, then canopy. The canopy top is **15–18 m**, not the 40 m cathedral
of Amazonian imagery. An LAI of 3.3 is a moderately dense canopy, not a
blackout.

**And a quarter to a half of it is bare in the dry season** — which is the
season the crisp light belongs to. A dry-season Petén scene is a
half-open canopy with light coming through, not a green wall. *(The
25–50% figure is attested; applying it to a dry-season scene is my
**inference**.)*

cream."[^123] Grade: **attested, tertiary** — note these conflict with
the 15–25 m canopy measured at Calakmul, which is what a Petén scene
should actually look like; the 38–45 m figures are maxima from elsewhere
in the species' ranges.

### Succession: how growth takes the stone back

| Fact | Figure | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Regrowth height after 5–7 years | 4–7 m | attested[^120] |
| Stem density ≥ 2 cm DBH at 5 years | ~22,000 /ha | attested[^120] |
| Stem density ≥ 2 cm DBH at 50 years | ~4,000 /ha | attested[^120] |
| Stems ≥ 25 cm DBH at 5 years | 0 /ha | attested[^120] |
| Stems ≥ 25 cm DBH at 50 years | 218 /ha | attested[^120] |
| Basal area, young forest (<4–6 yr) → old growth (30–50 yr) | 7.6 → 22.7 m²/ha (this study); 3.3–32.6 m²/ha across the range | attested[^120] |
| Time to return to secondary forest after cultivation | about 20 years of fallowing | attested[^120] |
| Late-successional species present | absent in the first 5–10 years; coexisting with pioneers only at 15–50 years | attested[^120] |

**Pioneers — what colonises bare ground and bare stone first:** *Trema
micrantha*, *Cecropia peltata*, *Solanum erianthum*, *Hampea trilobata*
— "short-lived, fast-growing, and shade-intolerant species that produce
small seeds that are dispersed mostly by the wind and birds."[^120]
*Cecropia obtusifolia* appears in the Calakmul flora list too.[^120]

**Late-successional:** *Brosimum alicastrum*, *Pimenta dioica*, *Talisia
olivaeformis*, *Manilkara zapota*.[^120]

**Persistent, dominant:** *Bursera simaruba*, *Malmea depressa*,
*Pouteria campechiana*, *Dendropanax arboreus*; persistent,
non-dominant: *Guettarda combsii*, *Simarouba glauca*, *Piscidia
piscipula*, *Coccoloba spicata*.[^120]

**Palms** of the peninsula, by use: roofing — *Sabal yapa*, *Sabal
mauritiiformis*; handicraft — *Cryosophila argentea*, *Desmoncus
quasillarius*; ornamental — *Chamaedorea seifrizii*.[^120]

Much of the regrowth is **resprouting, not seedlings**: of the pioneer
species recorded, 19 regenerated from stump regrowth against 6 from
seed; of the non-dominant persistent species, 37 against 5.[^120] That
matters visually — resprouts come up as multiple stems from one stool,
not as single saplings.

**Staging a ruin by age, from these figures (inferred):** a wall cleared
five years ago carries a dense thicket of thin pioneer stems 4–7 m tall
and no large trees at all; at fifty years there are 218 trees per hectare
over 25 cm thick, with roots into the masonry, and the thicket has
thinned to a quarter of its stem count. That is the difference between
"recently abandoned" and "swallowed," and it is authorable as two
parameter sets.

### What the botany sources do not have

Honestly marked, because an artist will want them: **no per-species
height or DBH** in the field studies; **no leaf size, leaf area, specific
leaf area, leaf-shape term, or leaf colour for any species**; **no month
in which any species drops its leaves**; **no epiphyte, liana, bromeliad
or strangler-fig data at all** (the Calakmul study measured only trees,
shrubs and palms at DBH ≥ 5 cm, which structurally excludes them); **no
canopy-closure time**; **no bark colour for any species**, including
*Bursera simaruba*, whose peeling red bark is its most recognisable
feature. All **unknown** from what I read.

The authority to go to for leaf and bark morphology is Pennington &
Sarukhán, *Árboles Tropicales de México* (3rd ed., 2005), which the
Calakmul secondary-forest study itself cites as its identification
reference.[^120]

### What this does to the current lighting

The board is lit by a sun at **about 42°, described as "late-afternoon,"
from the far left**.[^6] The tables say 42° at these latitudes is
**09:00 or 15:00** — mid-morning or mid-afternoon — in every season except
midwinter, where it never reaches 42° at all. True late afternoon, 17:00,
puts the sun at **14–21°**, a completely different image: shadows three
to four times longer, the sun nearly in frame, raking light across every
horizontal, and a sun colour far below the current 4500 K.

Two coherent ways to resolve it, both cheap:

1. **Keep 42° and stop calling it late afternoon.** It is a mid-afternoon
   sun, which is the right choice for a top-down readable board: long
   enough shadows to model the carving, short enough not to swallow
   cells. Note it in the art-direction page and move on.
2. **Make it actually late afternoon**: drop the sun to about 20°, swing
   the azimuth to ~290° (June) or ~243° (December), lengthen the shadows,
   warm the sun well below 4500 K, and accept that pieces will cast
   shadows across neighbouring cells. The more dramatic image, and the
   more expensive one to keep readable.

Either is defensible; the present pairing of a 42° altitude with a
"late-afternoon" description is the one that is not.

The azimuth is worth a second look. "From the far left" is a staging
choice, not a historical one, and nothing here argues against it. But if
the board is read as north-up — which a Mexica precinct invites, since
its whole iconographic programme is a north–south split — then a sun from
the left is a sun from the west, which pins the scene to the afternoon
and makes shadow direction and sun azimuth checkable against each other.

### Haze and aerial perspective

| Quantity | Mexico City | Palenque | Tikal | Los Tuxtlas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annual all-sky irradiance (kWh/m²/day) | 5.51 | 5.15 | 4.96 | 4.75 |
| Annual clear-sky irradiance | 6.92 | 6.54 | 6.53 | 6.36 |
| All-sky / clear-sky ratio (**inferred**) | 0.80 | 0.79 | 0.76 | 0.75 |
| Annual **direct-beam** irradiance | 4.53 | 4.16 | 3.65 | 3.36 |
| Annual cloud amount | 51.6% | 58.6% | 61.9% | 59.5% |
| Annual relative humidity | 65.8% | 79.9% | 82.7% | 82.5% |

All attested except the ratio row.[^3]

The useful contrast is the **direct-beam** row, not the total. Tikal
receives about 19% less direct beam than Mexico City while receiving only
10% less total light (**inferred** from the two attested rows). The
difference is scattered light: humidity near 83% all year, cloud near
62%, and the result is that a larger share of the illumination arrives
from the whole sky dome rather than from the sun disc.

For a renderer that is a concrete recipe:

| | Highland (Option A) | Lowland (Option B) |
|---|---|---|
| Sun (direct) | stronger, harder-edged shadows | weaker relative to ambient; softer shadow edges |
| Sky / ambient fill | low — shadows keep shape and go cool and deep | higher — shadows fill in, less contrast between lit and unlit |
| Aerial perspective | long range; mountains legible at tens of kilometres | short range; visible desaturation within a few hundred metres |
| Colour of the haze | thin, cool, slightly violet at distance | warm-grey to green-grey, picking up light bounced off the canopy |
| Best season for a crisp scene | dry season, any month; cloud bottoms at 36% in January | **March–April only** |
| Worst season | July, cloud 75.9% | September, cloud 74.1%, rain 11.5 mm/day |

The board currently runs low sky ambient "so the shadows have
shape."[^6] That is the **highland** recipe applied to a lowland material
set. If the lowland reading is kept, the sky fill should go up and the
shadow contrast down — and the compensating readability then has to come
from the rim light and the pieces' own emission, which the board already
has.

One more directly usable figure: the plaza, when new, was polished white
plaster that in full sunlight would have been "blinding," and the glare
"would have made users extremely uncomfortable."[^17] A plaza brighter
than the board is a real historical condition, not an error — but it
fights readability, which is why the project's rule that the plaza floor
sits at about 0.35 base colour against the board's 0.65[^6] is a sound
deliberate departure. It belongs in the departures record as one.

## Water features

**Highland.** A shallow alkaline lake, pale grey-green to colourless,
turbid in the north; stone-lined drains cut in pyroxene and lamprobolite
andesite; canals between artificial islands.[^1][^10]

**Lowland.** The Yucatán has "the almost complete absence of superficial
waterbodies such as rivers and streams"; its typical water is the
**cenote**, a sinkhole formed by the dissolution and collapse of
limestone, which usually has **vertical walls and a circular shape, with
varying depth reaching tens of metres**, and whose hydrology is stable
with low water-level fluctuation.[^124] Measured across ten cenotes in
Yucatán and Quintana Roo:[^124]

| Variable | Range | Mean |
|---|---|---|
| Secchi depth (visibility) | 2–15 m | 7.6 m |
| Water temperature | 24.9–27.7 °C | — |
| pH | 7.28–7.93 | — |
| Dissolved oxygen | 0.8–4.6 mg/L | 3.45 mg/L |
| Salinity | 0.03–0.07 ‰ | — |

A Secchi depth averaging 7.6 m is **very clear water** — the artist's
reference is a flooded limestone shaft with a visible bottom and strong
light shafts, not a murky pool. Most cenotes are oligotrophic to
mesotrophic; only about 14% are eutrophic.[^124] Open cenotes and closed
cavern types are different things visually and limnologically.

If the game wants water near a lowland temple, a cenote with vertical
walls and clear water is the attested feature. A river beside a Petén
temple is not.

## Accuracy checklist

The errors below are the ones that are easy to make from this page's
material and hard to see once made. Each is a factual slip, not a matter
of taste.

| Error | Why it is wrong | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| Attributing Maya material to the Mexica, or the reverse | different regions, different geology, different centuries. Mexica building is volcanic stone under stucco in a highland lake basin; Maya building is limestone veneer over a lime-concrete core in lowland forest | every fact on this page is labelled with its site; carry the label |
| Using the five-colour palette for a Maya surface, or the Bonampak twenty for a Mexica one | the five-colour restriction is specific to Mexica monumental sculpture and mural painting;[^4] Bonampak's painters used twenty distinguishable tones including greens[^5] | pick the palette that goes with the register |
| Calling any blue "Maya blue" | Maya blue is a specific palygorskite–indigo compound, identified by diffractometry on the Tlaltecuhtli monolith[^4] and, hedged, at Lamanai;[^114] Lothrop's word for the blue he saw at Tulum is "turquoise blue"[^110] | name the pigment only where a source identifies it |
| Mixing periods within one structure — a Puuc boot-vault on a Tulum façade, *talud-tablero* on a Petén temple, a corbelled vault on a Late Classic building | centuries and hundreds of kilometres apart; Late Classic vaulting uses specialised stones and is explicitly *not* corbelled[^34] | the period column in each table |
| Transplanting the Uxmal vault figures onto another site | every precise vault number here (64°, 2.75 m spring, 2.68 m span, 70 cm wall, 46 cm capstone span, 2° end-vault lean) is one surveyed room of one Classic Puuc palace[^85] | quote them as Uxmal, House of the Turtles, room 1 |
| Presenting a reconstruction as a measurement | the Templo Mayor's 36 m and 45 m are both reconstructions from stumps left at floor level;[^9][^10] several Tikal terrace counts are marked conjectural by their own excavator;[^24] plaza datum levels in the Tikal figures are estimated, not surveyed | say "reconstructed" where the source does |
| Quoting a measured figure without its caveat | the one measured Tikal batter, 0.06 m in 1.00 m, is suspected root-heave damage rather than design[^23] | carry the caveat or drop the figure |
| Treating the ruin state as the built state | grey weathered stone is the modern condition; Loten calls restored masonry "an entirely false impression" of the original[^17] | decide which moment the scene shows, and build to that moment's logic |
| Inventing glyph-like ornament | Maya writing is a decipherable script, so invented glyphs read as nonsense to anyone who reads it, the way invented Latin would | use non-writing ornament — step-fret, chevron, woven mat, disc-and-bar — or real glyphs checked by an epigrapher |
| Filling a gap from memory | this page marks what is unknown; the gaps are real gaps in the record, not gaps in the research | see [What this page does not know](#what-this-page-does-not-know) and the departures record |

## Rights and law

Two constraints, neither optional.

**1. Mexican federal law.** Under the *Ley Federal sobre Monumentos y
Zonas Arqueológicos, Artísticos e Históricos*, archaeological monuments,
movable and immovable, are "propiedad de la Nación, inalienables e
imprescriptibles" (Article 27), and **"Para la reproducción de monumentos
arqueológicos, históricos o artísticos, con fines comerciales, se
requerirá permiso del Instituto competente"** — reproduction of
archaeological monuments for commercial purposes requires INAH's
permission (Article 17).[^119] Movable archaeological monuments also may
not be transported, exhibited or reproduced without that permission
(Article 29).

For this project that means: **do not model a recognisable real
monument.** A 3D Coyolxauhqui disc, a recognisable Castillo, or a
traceable copy of a specific stela or serpent head is a commercial
reproduction of a Mexican archaeological monument. Original architecture
designed from the principles and dimensions on this page is not. The
project's existing rule — everything generated from parameters, nothing
copied — already lands on the right side of this; this page is the reason
it has to stay there.

Article 17 covers *reproduction of a monument*, not the use of a place
name, so calling a level "Palenque" is not caught by it. That is a
separate question — designation and trademark, outside this page's
competence and outside mine.

**2. Reference images.** Every image used as reference must have its
holder, identifier, date, place and rights status recorded before it is
looked at, and nothing whose rights are unknown may be shipped or copied.
The material-references list in
[Art direction](../explanation/art-direction.md) names its sources and
states that none is copied, traced or shipped, which is the right
pattern. One thing to tighten: the Texas Tech archive photographs cited
there should carry their rights status explicitly, not just a URL.

None of the sources quoted on this page is an image, and nothing here is
an instruction to copy anything.
